# Problem

Someone saving for a car or a laptop can know exactly what they want and still struggle to protect the money for it. A shared balance blurs the purpose of each deposit, unrestricted withdrawals leave the commitment easy to reverse, and small changes in a number may say little about the outcome being built. Behavioral research gives reasons to address these problems, while leaving the impact of NabungFi itself to be measured.

## 1. Pooled money can weaken a savings boundary

Soman and Cheema (2011) studied 146 workers in rural India. Money earmarked for savings was placed in one sealed envelope or divided between two. Over 14 weeks, average savings were 241 rupees with one envelope and 414 rupees with two: approximately **72% more saved in the partitioned condition**.

The same study found higher average savings with photographs of participants' children on the envelopes: 350 versus 304 rupees. The photographs reminded households why the money had been set aside. These were cash-envelope interventions, not digital vaults, and these figures are not measured NabungFi results. Source: [Soman and Cheema, Journal of Marketing Research](https://www.russellsage.org/sites/all/files/u137/jmr-C-s014-s021-online.pdf).

## 2. An intention does not restrict a withdrawal

A label in a wallet or spreadsheet still leaves the money available to spend. A voluntary commitment account adds a restriction selected in advance. In the Philippines, the SEED savings product prevented withdrawals until a chosen amount or date was reached, while paying the same interest rate as an ordinary savings account.

J-PAL's evaluation summary reports **82% higher average savings balances after one year** for the group offered the product relative to the comparison group. Only 28.4% of the 710 people offered SEED opened an account. The finding supports commitment products for some savers; it does not establish that everyone benefits from locking money or that NabungFi will reproduce that increase. Source: [J-PAL, Commitment Savings Products in the Philippines](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/commitment-savings-products-philippines?lang=en).

## 3. Progress can be difficult to connect to the goal

Cheema and Bagchi (2011) found that making goal progress easier to visualize could increase effort and commitment, including commitment toward savings. In their experiments, the benefit appeared when participants were close to the target. Source: [Cheema and Bagchi, Journal of Marketing](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jm.75.2.109).

This supports investigating visible progress, rather than claiming that numeric balances cause saving failure. NabungFi's construction model is a specific product hypothesis: seeing an object take shape may help users connect each deposit with their chosen outcome. Its effect on repeat deposits and goal completion has not yet been measured.

## Separation should not create competing priorities

Separating funds is different from encouraging more simultaneous goals. Soman and Zhao (2011) found that, under certain conditions, presenting one savings goal produced greater saving than presenting several competing goals. More goals can introduce trade-offs and delay action. Source: [Soman and Zhao, Journal of Marketing Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmr.10.0250).

The design implication is to make each goal's purpose and next action clear, without suggesting that creating more goals automatically improves savings.

## Wallets and networks add another bookkeeping problem

A wallet balance does not explain which part belongs to a car, a laptop or another commitment. Multiple addresses and network-specific balances add another layer of bookkeeping. Each goal needs its own target, reserves and completion decision.

Suppose a car has 80 USDC saved and a laptop has 20 USDC saved. A portfolio total of 100 USDC does not mean the laptop’s 100-USDC target is reached. Treating shared totals as goal eligibility would weaken the original commitment and let one goal borrow another goal’s reserves.

Even within one goal, a visible total is only an observation. One selected chain might still be waiting for a message or a verified reserve. Users need a clear distinction between reaching the target, verifying completion and receiving funds through a claim.
